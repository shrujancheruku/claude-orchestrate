# Evaluation methodology: evidence for orchestrating AI coding agents

Research note for [#609](https://github.com/shrujancheruku/claude-orchestrate/issues/609), a child of [map #588](https://github.com/shrujancheruku/claude-orchestrate/issues/588). Read 2026-11-10 from primary sources (arXiv full texts where a figure is quoted, benchmark maintainers' own pages, vendors' own posts). Anything read from an abstract page only, or from a search snippet, is marked "(abstract)" or "(secondary)". Area prefix `EV`.

**Summary.** (1) Single-run agent scores are noise at the scale orchestrate cares about: ten independent runs of six agent configurations on SWE-bench Verified (N=500) spread single-run pass@1 by 2.2 to 6.0 points, with run-to-run standard deviations of 1.0 to 1.8 points even at temperature 0, so a 2-point difference needs about 9 to 13 runs per arm to detect at 80% power, and a 1-point difference about 36 (Bjarnason et al. 2026). (2) The harness is a bigger lever than the model: with the model fixed, changing the scaffold moved SWE-bench Pro by 9.5 points and a 100-task Verified subset by 8.5 to 13 points, against 2.5 to 5 points for changing the model inside one harness (Zhang et al. 2026); scaffold choice therefore has to be an explicit, frozen variable in any A/B. (3) The public benchmarks orchestrate might borrow are each compromised in a known way: SWE-bench's issues predate model cutoffs (94%+), 32.67% of one agent's "solved" patches had the solution in the issue text (Aleithan et al. 2024), 24.4% of SWE-bench Verified leaderboard entries changed under stricter tests (Yu et al. 2025), file-path recall from issue text alone reaches 76% on SWE-bench repos vs 53% elsewhere (Liang et al. 2025), and the same agent that scores 43.2% on Verified scores 19.25% on post-2024 issues (Zhang et al. 2025); the maintainers' answers are held-out and live sets (SWE-bench Pro, SWE-bench-Live, LiveCodeBench) and a fixed minimal harness (Bash Only, Terminal-Bench's five-trial rule). (4) Cost belongs on the same axis as accuracy: simple retry/warming baselines matched or beat agent architectures on HumanEval at 1/50 of LATS's cost (Kapoor et al. 2024), the most expensive model was on the cost-accuracy frontier for 1 of 9 benchmarks (Kapoor et al. 2025), and verifier-filtered best-of-n has an optimal n "often fewer than 10" because false positives accumulate (Stroebl et al. 2024); pass^k, not pass@k, is the reliability number for an unattended pipeline (Yao et al. 2024). (5) Field metrics are thin and confounded: merge rate for agentic PRs ranges 38% to 65% by agent against 76.8% for humans in one dataset (Li et al. 2025) and 83.8% in another (Watanabe et al. 2025); each reviewer comment lowers an agentic PR's merge odds 2.8% while raising a human PR's 2.7% (Yoshioka et al. 2026); the only causal evidence on developer-level speed is three RCTs that disagree in sign (+26%, +21% n.s., -19%), and the one large quasi-experiment on repo-level velocity finds the gain gone after two months while static-analysis warnings stay up 30% (He et al. 2026). Nothing in the sources measures an orchestrator of parallel agents end to end; orchestrate's own `profile.py compare` (20 merged tickets, raw single-run rates) is an engineering sanity check, not a trial.

## Sources

### 1. Bjarnason, Silva and Monperrus (2026). On Randomness in Agentic Evals. arXiv:2602.07150. Grade A

- Design: controlled repeated-run measurement; 10 independent runs of each of 6 agent configurations (3 models x 2 scaffolds), each at its suggested temperature and at 0, so 12 configurations and 120 runs; 60,000 trajectories, 25.58B tokens.
- Population: Qwen3-32B, DeepSWE-preview, Devstral-2; scaffolds nano-agent and R2E-Gym; SWE-bench Verified, N=500.
- Results: single-run pass@1 estimates "vary by 2.2 to 6.0 percentage points" across runs of the same configuration (Table 1); standard deviations at temperature 0 of 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8 points. Runs diverge "within the first 1% of tokens" (median first divergence at token 5 to 56). pass@5 exceeds pass@1 by up to 24.9 points while pass^5 falls well below it (DeepSWE on R2E-Gym: pass@1 34.4%, pass@5 52.9%, pass^5 15.5%; Devstral-2 on nano-agent: 63.5 / 76.2 / 49.1). Power table (Table 2, 80% power, p<0.05): detecting a 2-point improvement needs 2 runs per arm at sigma 0.7, 9 at sigma 1.5, 13 at sigma 1.8; a 1-point improvement at sigma 1.5 needs 36; a 10-point improvement needs 1. Formula: n >= 2((z_a/2 + z_b) / (delta/sigma))^2.
- Limitations: open-weight models only; two scaffolds; the abstract's "exceeding 1.5 points" is true for 3 of 6 temperature-0 cells.
- For orchestrate: the baseline-vs-trial comparison `profile.py compare` makes today is one run against one run. The arms would need to be repeated on the same ticket set, and the number of repeats chosen from the observed between-run sigma, before a difference of a few points means anything.

### 2. Zhang, Wang, Ge, Xu, Hamm and Reddy (2026). Stop Comparing LLM Agents Without Disclosing the Harness. arXiv:2605.23950. Grade B

- Design: leaderboard re-analysis plus a controlled 3 models x 3 harnesses factorial on a 100-task SWE-bench Verified subset, two runs per cell, shared task order, Docker, 50-step budget, 120 s per step.
- Population: GPT-5.4, Kimi K2.6, GLM-5.1 in a Minimal, Improved and Full harness; leaderboard data for Claude Opus 4.5 and others.
- Results: with Claude Opus 4.5 fixed, SWE-bench Pro moved from 45.9% (SEAL scaffold) to 55.4% (Claude Code), a 9.5-point harness-only spread, while six frontier models under the one SEAL scaffold spanned 4.9 points (41.0 to 45.9). HAL data: Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored 68% in SWE-Agent and 34% in HAL Generalist on SWE-bench Verified Mini (34-point gap); GPT-5 Medium 46% vs 12%. In the factorial, harness variance exceeded model variance 7.80x in aggregate (8.72x and 6.76x in the two individual runs); changing the harness moved GLM-5.1 by 13.0 points and the others by 8.5, changing the model inside one harness moved scores 3.0, 2.5 and 5.0 points; 6 ranking reversals in 9 model-pair/harness-pair comparisons. Adding one search subagent on identical infrastructure added 2.1 to 2.2 points and flipped a model ordering.
- Limitations: two runs per cell, no CIs; the 7.80x ratio is explicitly not claimed to be universal.
- For orchestrate: the plugin is a harness. A result for "Sonnet worker vs Opus worker" is uninterpretable unless the prompts, tools, step budgets and reviewer tiers were frozen; equally, a prompt or tool change to a role can be worth more than a model swap and should be trialled as its own arm.

### 3. Miller (2024). Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations. arXiv:2411.00640. Grade B

- Design: statistical methodology with worked examples on real eval data.
- Results: SE of a mean score is sqrt(Var(s)/n), Bernoulli SE sqrt(p(1-p)/n). When questions come in related groups, clustered SEs: on DROP the clustered SE was 1.34 vs 0.44 naive (3.05x), "over 3X larger". Resampling each question K times reduces variance by (1+2/K)/3 in the uniform-difficulty binary case (K=2 cuts a third, K=4 half) with a ceiling of 2/3; pooling all KN answers as independent "will be inconsistent". For two models, test question-level paired differences: Var(paired) = Var(unpaired) - 2Cov/n; at correlation 0.5 the variance falls by a third, a "free" reduction. Do not lower temperature to shrink variance. Power: n = (z_a/2 + z_b)^2 (omega^2 + sigma_A^2/K_A + sigma_B^2/K_B) / delta^2; a 3-point MDE at 80% power needs about 969 questions, so "new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions"; with n=198, raising K from 1 to 10 cuts the MDE from 13.2% to 7.5%.
- Limitations: CLT framing; see Bowyer et al. for small n.
- For orchestrate: tickets come in correlated clusters (one parent, one subsystem), so SEs must be clustered by parent or path; the same ticket set run under both arms is the paired design the paper recommends; the orchestrator should log per-ticket outcomes, not just session totals, so pairing is possible.

### 4. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova (2025). Position: Don't Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints. ICML 2025 (spotlight position paper). arXiv:2503.01747. Grade B (abstract)

- Results: CLT-based error bars on small benchmarks are "usually dramatically underestimating uncertainty"; the paper proposes frequentist and Bayesian alternatives (library `bayes_evals`).
- For orchestrate: a trial of 20 to 50 merged tickets is squarely in the regime the paper says CLT intervals mislead in; any interval orchestrate reports at that size should be a Beta/Wilson-type interval and labelled as wide.

### 5. Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein (METR) (2025). Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity. arXiv:2507.09089. Grade A (abstract)

- Design: RCT, each task randomised to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed.
- Population: 16 experienced developers, 246 tasks in mature open-source projects (average 5 years' prior experience on the project); mainly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet.
- Results: AI increased completion time by 19%; developers forecast a 24% reduction and afterwards believed they had been 20% faster; economics and ML experts forecast 39% and 38% reductions.
- Limitations: 16 developers; early-2025 tools; the abstract page gives no CI.
- For orchestrate: self-report and expert intuition were wrong in sign; any claim about orchestrate's effect has to come from the record, not the operator's impression. Also the one setting most like orchestrate's (expert maintainers, mature repos) is the one where AI slowed people down.

### 6. Paradis et al. (Google) (2024). How much does AI impact development speed? An enterprise-based randomized controlled trial. arXiv:2410.12944. Grade A

- Design: RCT, 96 Google engineers randomised 48/48, 93 completed; one C++ task (implement a logging service from a 10-file, 474-line change list; done when tests pass; designed for under 3 hours).
- Results: mean 96 min with AI (SE 9.3) vs 114 min without (SE 8.1); t-test on log time t(83.6)=2.11, p=.038; adjusted estimate "about 21%" faster, beta=-0.24, 95% CI [-0.51, 0.03], p=0.086 in the best-fit model.
- Limitations: one task, one company's internal tools, summer 2024; the adjusted CI crosses zero.
- For orchestrate: even a 96-person RCT on one task leaves a CI from 51% faster to 3% slower. Per-ticket effects on orchestrate's scale will not reach significance; the honest unit of claim is a direction with an interval.

### 7. Cui, Demirer, Jaffe, Musolff, Peng and Salz (2025). The Effects of Generative AI on High-Skilled Work: Evidence from Three Field Experiments with Software Developers. Management Science, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535 (SSRN 4945566). Grade A (secondary: SSRN abstract via search snippet)

- Design: three company-run RCTs (Microsoft, Accenture, a Fortune 100 manufacturer) of GitHub Copilot access; 4,867 developers pooled.
- Results: 26.08% more completed tasks (SE 10.3%); larger gains for less experienced developers.
- Limitations: Copilot autocomplete, 2023 to 2024; outcome is tasks completed, not quality.
- For orchestrate: the strongest positive causal number in the literature is still a 2.5-sigma result from nearly 5,000 developers. It sets the scale of effect one may expect per-developer, and shows that "tasks completed" without a quality arm is how the optimistic numbers arise.

### 8. He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner and Vasilescu (2026). Speed at the Cost of Quality: How Cursor AI Increases Short-Term Velocity and Long-Term Complexity in Open-Source Projects. MSR 2026. arXiv:2511.04427. Grade A

- Design: difference-in-differences with matched controls, panel GMM; 806 Cursor-adopting repositories (adoption detected from `.cursorrules` or `.cursor` commits, Jan 2024 to Mar 2025) against 1,380 matched controls (the methods describe 1:3 matching; the two counts are not reconciled in the paper); SonarQube metrics, GHArchive activity.
- Results: lines added +28.58% (+/-13.7) overall; by month, commits +55.4% in month 1 and +14.5% in month 2, lines added +281.3% then +48.4%, then no significant gain: "gains dissipate after two months". Static-analysis warnings +30.26% (+/-6.66) and code complexity +41.64% (+/-7.62), both persistent (the text elsewhere gives 25.1% for complexity). GMM: a 100% rise in complexity cuts future lines added 64.5%, in warnings 50.3%.
- Limitations: adoption proxy; observational; open-source only.
- For orchestrate: throughput measured over a few weeks overstates the effect; a credible self-measurement needs a quality series (static-analysis warnings, complexity, escaped defects) running months past the throughput window.

### 9. Li, Zhang and Hassan (2025). The Rise of AI Teammates in Software Engineering (SE) 3.0 (the AIDev dataset). arXiv:2507.15003. Grade A (large field dataset)

- Design: mining study; 456,535 agentic PRs (Codex 411,621; Devin 24,893; Copilot 16,531; Cursor 1,981; Claude Code 1,509) across 61k repositories; the acceptance analysis uses AIDev-pop (repos over 500 stars; 7,122 agentic and 6,618 human PRs).
- Results (Table 5, AIDev-pop): accepted / rejected / median hours to close when accepted: human 76.8% / 16.1% / 3.9 h; Codex 65.3% / 21.3% / 0.3 h; Devin 48.9% / 48.4% / 2.2 h; Copilot 38.2% / 32.1% / 17.2 h; Cursor 51.4% / 21.5% / 2.4 h; Claude Code 52.5% / 32.7% / 6.9 h. Review: 75.3% of human PRs and 58.2% of agentic PRs show no explicit review; bots were involved in 20.1% of agentic PRs vs 10.0% of human. Documentation PRs are accepted more often than human ones (Codex 88.6%, Claude Code 85.7%, human 76.5%). The text's acceptance figures (Codex 64%, Copilot 35%) disagree slightly with Table 5.
- Limitations: "accepted" and "merged" used interchangeably; accepted plus rejected do not sum to 100% and the remainder is unexplained; no PR-size or review-round figures.
- For orchestrate: merge rate and time-to-close are the two field metrics with a published comparison population; both are stratified by agent and by task type (docs vs feat vs fix), so orchestrate's numbers must be stratified the same way before any comparison.

### 10. Yoshioka et al. (2026). Let's Make Every Pull Request Meaningful: An Empirical Analysis of Developer and Agentic Pull Requests. MSR 2026. arXiv:2601.18749. Grade A

- Design: regression on 64 features in six families; 33,596 agentic PRs with full metadata (Codex 21,799; Copilot 4,970; Devin 4,827; Cursor 1,541; Claude Code 459) and 6,618 human PRs from AIDev.
- Results: each additional reviewer comment raises merge odds 2.7% for human PRs and lowers them 2.8% for agentic PRs. Submitter attributes carry 69.00% of the model chi-square for human PRs and 76.15% for agentic; when submitter and integrator are the same person the odds multiply by about 219x (human) and 5,419x (agentic); 77.51% of merged agentic PRs were self-integrated vs 57.63% of human (z=-29.38, p<0.001). 3.7% of merged agentic PRs had more than three reviewers vs 5.8% of unmerged.
- Limitations: observational; "same user" dominance means most merged agentic PRs were merged by the person who launched the agent.
- For orchestrate: review comments on an agentic PR predict non-merge, so "rounds to merge" and "comments per PR" are legitimate quality proxies in orchestrate's record, and a falling merge rate with rising comment counts is the expected signature of a worse worker. Also a self-measurement where the operator merges their own agent's PRs is the 5,419x population; it says little about third-party acceptance.

### 11. Mazloomzadeh, Morovati and Khomh (2026). How Do AI Coding Agents Contribute to Software Development? An Empirical Study of Agentic Pull Requests. arXiv:2607.21832. Grade A (large field dataset; first 100k characters read)

- Design: 220,612 closed PRs from 489 Python repositories, 9,428 agentic (7,293 merged, 2,135 rejected); GLMM with repository random effects; quarterly analysis over 309 repositories.
- Results: raw agentic merge rate 77.4%; model-adjusted by quarter 65.4 / 63.7 / 68.2 / 67.7% (differences not significant) against raw human 85.0 / 84.0 / 85.6 / 85.3%. Adjusted per agent: Claude 84.3%, Codex 73.5%, Cursor 63.9%, Copilot 59.6%, Devin 43.0% (raw: Codex 90.0%, Claude 75.8%, Copilot 69.4%, Cursor 53.8%, Devin 37.8%). Claude vs Copilot OR 3.631 (p=0.001), vs Devin 7.098 (p<0.001), vs Codex 1.929 (p=0.178). Merge ratio by topic: CI, dependency, workflow and hook changes over 0.90; UI, logging, function implementation 0.60 to 0.80; LLM integration and model evaluation lowest.
- Limitations: raw and adjusted figures diverge widely (repository mix); RQ3 time-to-merge and bug-inducing results were in the unread tail.
- For orchestrate: merge rate is strongly a function of ticket type; a trial whose ticket mix shifts toward docs/dependency work will show a merge-rate gain that is mix, not workflow. `profile.py compare` already reweights by review tier; it should also reweight (or stratify) by ticket type.

### 12. Zhu et al. (2025). Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks (the ABC checklist). arXiv:2507.02825. Grade B

- Design: checklist (task validity T.1-T.10, outcome validity, reporting R.1-R.13) applied to 10 open-source agentic benchmarks; case study on CVE-Bench.
- Results: 7 of 10 benchmarks have task-validity flaws, 7 of 10 outcome-validity flaws, all 10 reporting limitations, about 80% "fail to acknowledge weaknesses". SWE-bench Verified: insufficient unit tests; 24% of the top 50 leaderboard positions are incorrect (citing 5.2% of tasks passed without a correct patch). tau-bench: a trivial agent returning empty responses scores 38%, above a GPT-4o agent at 35%. KernelBench correctness overestimated about 31 points; OSWorld 13 of 46 Chrome tasks broken. ABC cut CVE-Bench overestimation by 33 points absolute. Reporting items include statistical significance (R.10) and trivial-agent baselines (R.12-R.13).
- Limitations: benchmark counts inconsistent between sections (10, 17, 25).
- For orchestrate: any planted-defect or ticket benchmark orchestrate builds (#589) should pass T.5 (agent isolated from ground truth), T.7 (ground truth verified), R.10 (significance) and R.12 (a trivial baseline: e.g. a "merge if CI green, no reviewer" arm).

### 13. Kapoor, Stroebl, Siegel, Nadgir and Narayanan (2024). AI Agents That Matter. arXiv:2407.01502. Grade B

- Design: cost-controlled re-evaluation, five runs per agent, 95% CIs from Student's t; survey of 17 benchmarks' hold-outs.
- Results (Table A1, HumanEval, 164 problems, mean of five runs; total cost per full run): LATS (GPT-4) 88.0% at $134.50; LDB (GPT-4+3.5) 91.0% at $2.19; Reflexion (GPT-4) 87.8% at $3.90; warming baseline 93.2% at $2.45; retry baseline 92.0% at $2.51; escalation 85.0% at $0.27; GPT-4 zero-shot 89.6% at $1.93. "No significant accuracy difference between our warming strategy and the best-performing agent architecture"; LATS costs "over 50 times more". Joint cost-accuracy optimisation on HotPotQA cut variable cost 53% (GPT-3.5) and 41% (Llama-3-70B) at similar accuracy. 7 of 17 benchmarks have no hold-out at all. Many published scores "were above the maximum of five runs" in reproduction. SWE-bench at $4 per task could cost "over USD 8,000" per run, which is why repeated runs and error bars are rare. Recommends reporting token counts so costs can be re-priced.
- Limitations: 2024 models; HumanEval is saturated.
- For orchestrate: the retry/warming result is the direct analogue of "re-dispatch a failed worker once" beating a heavier scaffold; the plugin's proxy-$-per-merged-ticket is the right axis, and token counts per role (which `profile.py` records) are what make the figure re-priceable.

### 14. Kapoor et al. (2025). Holistic Agent Leaderboard: The Missing Infrastructure for AI Agent Evaluation. arXiv:2510.11977. Grade B

- Design: 21,730 rollouts, about $40,000, 9 models x 9 benchmarks, 10 scaffolds; LLM-aided review of 1,634 transcripts from 36 model-scaffold pairs.
- Results: Claude Opus 4.1 (most expensive) on the cost-accuracy frontier for 1 of 9 benchmarks; Gemini 2.0 Flash on it for 7 of 9; fewer than a third of models on the frontier per benchmark on average. In 21 of 36 combinations more reasoning effort gave equal or lower accuracy. Task-specific scaffolds beat the generalist on 11 of 12 SWE-bench Verified Mini runs; the generalist was cheaper in 20 of 24 comparisons. Same task, scaffold swap: $171 vs $1,577 for about 2 points. Eight cases of agents finding gold answers online; hard-coded solutions to pass unit tests on CORE-Bench and SciCode; agents violated a benchmark instruction over 60% of the time in failed AssistantBench/CORE-Bench tasks. Verification steps raised success 13% to 87%; successful self-correction made success 1.5x to 4x more likely.
- Limitations: no run-to-run variance reported.
- For orchestrate: "higher effort is not better" is testable per role with the plugin's cost profile; transcript inspection for shortcutting (tests edited to pass, guard bypasses) should be part of any orchestrate trial, which the guard hook and run log partly give for free.

### 15. Aleithan, Xue, Mohajer, Nnorom, Uddin and Wang (2024). SWE-Bench+: Enhanced Coding Benchmark for LLMs. arXiv:2410.06992. Grade B (abstract)

- Design: manual audit of SWE-Agent+GPT-4's passing patches on SWE-bench (2,294 issues, 12 repos).
- Results: 32.67% of successful patches "involve cheating" (solution in the issue or comments); 31.08% "suspicious" because tests are weak; filtering both drops SWE-Agent+GPT-4 from 12.47% to 3.97% resolved; over 94% of issues predate model knowledge cutoffs; the same problems appear in Lite and Verified.
- For orchestrate: a ticket backlog written by the operator is the same hazard. Tickets that contain the patch, or whose acceptance tests are weak, inflate any workflow's score equally, so the trial ticket set must be audited for leaked solutions and test strength before use.

### 16. Liang, Garg and Zilouchian Moghaddam (2025). The SWE-Bench Illusion: When State-of-the-Art LLMs Remember Instead of Reason. arXiv:2506.12286. Grade B (abstract)

- Results: models identify the buggy file path from the issue text alone, no repository access, with up to 76% accuracy on SWE-bench Verified; up to 53% on repositories outside SWE-bench. Verbatim 5-gram reproduction of ground-truth functions up to 35% on SWE-bench Verified/Full vs up to 18% on other benchmarks.
- For orchestrate: a benchmark drawn from well-known public repos measures memory in part; orchestrate's own repos and fresh tickets are a better trial set for the workflow question even though they have no leaderboard.

### 17. Yu, Zhu, He and Kang (2025). UTBoost: Rigorous Evaluation of Coding Agents on SWE-Bench. ACL 2025. arXiv:2506.09289. Grade B (abstract)

- Results: 36 SWE-bench instances with insufficient tests; 345 patches wrongly labelled as passed; 40.9% of SWE-bench Lite and 24.4% of SWE-bench Verified leaderboard entries affected; 18 and 11 rank changes.
- For orchestrate: test-based verdicts are only as strict as the tests. An orchestrate trial scored on "CI green" inherits the repo's test weakness; an escaped-defect record over the following weeks is the correction.

### 18. Zhang et al. (Microsoft) (2025). SWE-bench Goes Live! (SWE-bench-Live). arXiv:2505.23419. Grade B

- Design: automated curation of 1,319 issues created since 2024 from 93 Python repositories, Docker per task; Lite subset of 300 (50 per month Oct 2024 to Mar 2025); planned monthly updates.
- Results: best Full score 19.25% (OpenHands + Claude 3.7 Sonnet); the same agent re-run on SWE-bench Verified under the same setup scored 43.20%, "more than twice". Instances from SWE-bench's original repositories resolved at 22.96% vs 18.89% elsewhere. Patches of fewer than five lines in one file: about 48% resolved; three or more files or over 100 lines: under 10%; seven or more files: never. Repos under 100 files often over 20%, over 500 files rarely over 5%.
- Limitations: instance counts for the repo-origin split are inconsistent between text and table.
- For orchestrate: the size-of-change gradient is steep, which supports sizing tickets small and the plugin's S/M/L review tiers as a stratification variable; a trial's difficulty mix must be reported.

### 19. Deng et al. (Scale AI) (2025). SWE-Bench Pro: Can AI Agents Solve Long-Horizon Software Engineering Tasks? arXiv:2509.16941. Grade B

- Design: 1,865 human-verified tasks from 41 repositories: public 731 (GPL/copyleft repos, chosen so the code is legally unattractive for training corpora), commercial 276 (results published, code private), held-out 858; SWE-Agent scaffold, 50-turn cap; gold tests re-run several times for flakiness; humans pruned irrelevant or over-broad fail-to-pass tests.
- Results (pass@1): public set Claude Sonnet 4.5 43.6%, Sonnet 4 42.7%, GPT-5 high 41.8%; commercial set Claude Opus 4.1 17.8%, GPT-5 high 15.7%, Gemini 2.5 Pro 10.1%, GPT-4o 3.6%. Removing the human-written requirement/interface augmentation drops GPT-5 from 25.9% to 8.4% and Opus 4.1 from 22.7% to 8.2% (Table 3). Failure modes (GPT-5 judge, 87% agreement with humans): Opus 4.1 wrong solution 35.9%, syntax 24.2%; Sonnet 4 context overflow 35.6%, endless file reading 17.0%.
- Limitations: no run count or variance reported; one scaffold.
- For orchestrate: the public-to-commercial gap (43.6% to 17.8%) is the clearest single number for how much public-repo scores overstate performance on private code, which is where orchestrate runs. The augmentation ablation is also evidence that ticket quality (requirements, interfaces) is worth about 3x in resolve rate.

### 20. Jain et al. (2024). LiveCodeBench: Holistic and Contamination Free Evaluation of Large Language Models for Code. arXiv:2403.07974. Grade B

- Design: release-dated problems from LeetCode, AtCoder, Codeforces (511 collected, 349 used after a Sep 2023 filter); four scenarios; "scrolling through time" evaluation on problems after a model's cutoff.
- Results: DeepSeek-Base-33B pass@1 falls from about 60 on May-2023 problems to about 0 on September-2023 LeetCode problems; Codestral 36.5 on May 2023 to Jan 2024 vs 28.3 from Feb 2024; GPT-4-O drops on post-November-2023 problems; the drop is mainly on LeetCode. HumanEval+ overfitting: DS-Ins-1.3B 59.8% on HumanEval+ vs 26.3% on LCB-Easy; HumanEval+/LCB-Easy correlation 0.72.
- For orchestrate: date-stamping is the cheap contamination control; a trial ticket set should be tickets filed after the models' cutoffs, which orchestrate's live backlog is by construction.

### 21. Yao, Shinn, Razavi and Narasimhan (Sierra) (2024). tau-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains. arXiv:2406.12045. Grade B

- Design: tool-agent-user simulation; at least 3 trials per task for main results, at least 40 for difficulty calibration.
- Results: pass^k defined as the probability all k independent trials succeed, estimated per task as C(c,k)/C(n,k) (pass@k is 1 - C(n-c,k)/C(n,k)); gpt-4o pass^1 61.2 (retail) and 35.2 (airline), pass^8 under 25% in retail. Motivation: deployed agents need consistency, "not just high average success".
- For orchestrate: a worker that merges a ticket 60% of the time is a worker that fails 2 of 5 unattended; pass^k over repeated dispatch of the same ticket is the reliability figure for a pipeline, and it needs the same multi-trial design as source 1.

### 22. Yang et al. (2024). SWE-agent: Agent-Computer Interfaces Enable Automated Software Engineering. NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2405.15793. Grade B

- Results (Appendix B.5, Table 10): six runs of SWE-agent+GPT-4 on SWE-bench Lite resolve 17.33, 18.00, 18.00, 18.67, 17.33, 18.33 (mean 17.94, s.d. 0.49); pass@k rises 17.94, 23.89, 27.35, 29.67, 31.33, 32.67 for k=1..6: "average performance variance is relatively low, though per-instance resolution can change considerably". Per-instance budget $4; results otherwise single-run pass@1; §B.2 reports success "uncorrelated to the issue age".
- For orchestrate: the earliest published run-to-run figure for a coding agent; even at s.d. 0.49 the set of tickets solved churns, so per-ticket outcomes between two arms will disagree for reasons unrelated to the arms.

### 23. Xia, Deng, Dunn and Zhang (2024). Agentless: Demystifying LLM-based Software Engineering Agents. arXiv:2407.01489. Grade B

- Results (SWE-bench Lite, Table 1): Agentless (GPT-4o) 32.00% at $0.70 and 78k tokens per problem; SWE-agent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) 23.00% at $1.62 and 521k tokens; SWE-agent (GPT-4o) 18.33% at $2.53; OpenDevin CodeAct v1.8 (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) 26.67% at $1.14; AutoCodeRover (GPT-4) 19.00% at $0.45. Lite-S removes 51 of 300 problems (exact patch in description 4.3%, misleading solution 5.0%, insufficient information 10.0%); sampling at temperature 0.8 with regression-test-filtered candidate selection; no repeated-run variance reported.
- For orchestrate: a fixed three-phase pipeline beat autonomous agents at a third of the cost in 2024, and its selection step (discard candidates that break existing tests, then pick) is the rejection-sampling pattern Anthropic later adopted (source 27). Both argue for scripts owning the verdict, as orchestrate does.

### 24. Brown et al. (2024). Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling. arXiv:2407.21787. Grade B (abstract); and Stroebl, Kapoor and Narayanan (2024). The Limits of Inference Scaling Through Resampling. arXiv:2411.17501. Grade B (abstract)

- Results: coverage (any-sample success) on SWE-bench Lite with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct rises from 15.9% at one sample to 56% at 250, roughly log-linear; majority voting and reward models "plateau beyond several hundred samples". Stroebl et al.: with an imperfect verifier (tests with false positives) resampling has an accuracy ceiling regardless of budget, weaker models cannot be scaled to match a stronger model's single sample, and "optimal sampling attempts are often fewer than 10, as the negative utility of false positives outweighs benefits"; false positives also show poorer style adherence.
- For orchestrate: parallel workers on one ticket with the test suite as the selector is a real paradigm, but its ceiling is the test suite's false-positive rate and its sweet spot is a handful of attempts, not many; the evidence is against a cheap-model-many-tries arm matching one strong-model attempt.

### 25. Erol, El, Suzgun, Yuksekgonul and Zou (2025). Cost-of-Pass: An Economic Framework for Evaluating Language Models. arXiv:2504.13359. Grade B (abstract)

- Results: cost-of-pass = expected monetary cost of obtaining one correct solution (cost per attempt / pass rate); the frontier cost-of-pass is the minimum over models including a human-expert baseline priced at the cost of hiring the expert; the frontier "roughly halved every few months" on complex quantitative tasks; "performance-oriented methods with marginal performance gains rarely justify the costs".
- For orchestrate: `profile.py`'s proxy $ per merged ticket is a cost-of-pass; the paper's extension is to divide by the pass rate of the whole pipeline (including abandoned tickets), which the plugin's "overhead" term partly does, and to compare against a human-hour baseline.

### 26. Miserendino, Wang, Patwardhan and Heidecke (OpenAI) (2025). SWE-Lancer: Can Frontier LLMs Earn $1 Million from Real-World Freelance Software Engineering? arXiv:2502.12115. Grade B

- Design: 1,488 Upwork tasks worth $1,000,000 (764 IC SWE worth $414,775; 724 SWE Manager worth $585,225); Diamond public set 502 tasks worth $500,800; end-to-end tests "triple-verified by professional software engineers"; single attempt at temperature 1.0, and the authors note "there can be significant variance between runs".
- Results: Claude 3.5 Sonnet IC SWE (Diamond) pass@1 26.2%, $58k of $236k (24.5% earn rate); SWE Manager 44.9%, $150k of $265k; overall Diamond 36.1%, $208,050 of $500,800 (41.5%); o1 (high) IC SWE 16.5%, $29k.
- For orchestrate: the one benchmark that prices tasks in dollars; earn rate weights hard tickets more than a count does. An orchestrate record could weight merged tickets by review tier or estimated hours the same way, and the paper's own caveat applies to every single-attempt number it reports.

### 27. Benchmark maintainers' own pages. Grade C

- Anthropic, "Introducing Claude 4" (2025), footnotes: SWE-bench Verified scores reported "out of the full 500 problems" with "the same simple scaffold" of a bash tool and a string-replacement file editor; the "high compute" numbers (79.4% Opus 4, 80.2% Sonnet 4) "sample multiple parallel attempts", "discard patches that break the visible regression tests in the repository, similar to the rejection sampling approach adopted by Agentless", then "use an internal scoring model to select the best candidate"; "Scores for OpenAI models are reported out of a 477 problem subset". - Epoch AI, SWE-bench Verified hub page (v2.0.3, 2026-03-06): runs 484 of 500 tasks, excluding 16 "that do not reliably run in our infrastructure"; one task's test list amended; token budget 2M uncached and 20M cached; a simple one-action-per-turn loop with bash, text_editor and apply_patch; cites a prior "error rate of 5-10%" for the dataset; the exclusions moved GPT-5 Mini's score from 59.9% to 59.8%; no run count or error bars published.
- Princeton SWE-bench Verified dataset card: "a subset of 500 samples from the SWE-bench test set, which have been human-validated for quality" and a pointer to OpenAI's post for the annotation process; annotator counts and the 0-3 severity rubric are not reproduced here because the primary post was unreachable.
- Terminal-Bench 2.0 (Merrill, Shaw, Carlini et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.11868, abstract): 89 tasks, each with its own environment, human-written solution and tests; frontier agents under 65%. Leaderboard rules (harborframework/terminal-bench-2-leaderboard dataset card): "Each task must be evaluated with a minimum of five trials. We recommend the -k 5 flag"; `timeout_multiplier` 1.0, no timeout or CPU/memory/storage overrides; "Agents cannot access the Terminal-Bench website or GitHub repository (reward hacking)"; the 2.0 announcement cites TB1.0's `download-youtube` task breaking when YouTube changed its anti-bot protections as the reason for heavier verification.
- Aider polyglot (aider.chat, 2024-12-21): 225 of 697 Exercism exercises in C++ 26, Go 39, Java 47, JavaScript 49, Python 34, Rust 30, keeping problems "solved by 3 or fewer" of seven top models; replaced the Python-only set because top scores "approached and then surpassed 80%"; launch scores o1 61.7%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 45.3%, GPT-4o 15.1%.
- SWE-bench "Bash Only" leaderboard (swebench.com): mini-SWE-agent with no extra tools so that models are compared "on equal footing" (secondary: the page returned 404 at one path; description from search results).
- For orchestrate: the maintainers themselves now (a) average or require multiple trials, (b) freeze the scaffold for model comparisons, (c) exclude or amend tasks their infrastructure cannot run and say so, and (d) forbid agents from reading the benchmark. Those four are the minimum for an orchestrate trial too: repeated runs, frozen roles, a published exclusion list, and a guard against the agent seeing the answer key.

### 28. Field reports. Grade C

- Faros AI, "The AI Productivity Paradox Report 2025": telemetry from over 10,000 developers in 1,255 teams; per team, the two lowest-adoption quarters vs the two highest: high-adoption developers "complete 21% more tasks" and "merge 98% more pull requests"; "PR review time increases 91%"; "a 9% increase in bugs per developer and a 154% increase in average PR size"; "no significant correlation between AI adoption and improvements at the company level" on throughput, DORA metrics or quality KPIs: "the gains observed in team behavior do not scale when aggregated". Faros's 2026 follow-up reports bugs per developer +54% and median review time +441.5% on 22,000 developers (secondary: search snippets).
- CodeRabbit (Loker 2025-12-17), State of AI vs Human Code Generation: 470 PRs (320 AI-co-authored, 150 human-only); "~1.7x more issues overall" (10.83 vs 6.45 per PR); security "up to 2.74x"; readability "more than 3x"; AI authorship inferred from co-author signals and the rest assumed human, which the report says it "cannot guarantee". The HOS repo's provenance note attributes a 1.5-2x security figure and Poisson CIs to this report; the blog page read here says 2.74x and "rate ratios" without CIs, so those details are unverified.
- Watanabe et al. (2025). On the Use of Agentic Coding: An Empirical Study of Pull Requests on GitHub. arXiv:2509.14745 (abstract): 567 Claude Code PRs in 157 projects; 83.8% merged; 54.9% of merged PRs merged without further modification; used most for refactoring, documentation, testing.
- Peralta et al. (2026). Why Are Agentic Pull Requests Merged or Rejected? MSR 2026. arXiv:2605.22534 (abstract): 11,048 closed agentic PRs, 717 inspected; of rejections 35.7% clear agentic failures, 31.2% workflow constraints, 33.1% no observable rationale, so "rejection outcomes substantially overstate agent error"; 15.4% of merged PRs needed explicit reviewer feedback or commits.
- Thurlow (2026), HOS finding "Dogfooding an oversight framework on itself systematically inflates the measured human-intervention rate": a framework whose protected surfaces are its own source files routes nearly every self-hosted PR to a human (seven of seven in one session), so friction rates measured on the framework's own repo are "systematically pessimistic" and the self-hosted sample "does not sample its own target population".
- For orchestrate: PR size and review time are the field signals that moved most under AI adoption, and a third of rejections carry no reason, so "rejected" is a poor defect proxy; orchestrate's record should separate rejected-for-defect from rejected-for-process. The HOS point applies directly: a trial run on claude-orchestrate itself (sensitive-path prompts, plugin files) is not the consumer population; Issho or another consumer repo is.

## Candidate paradigms

These are the evaluation and orchestration paradigms this area's evidence describes; the orchestration ones are marked.

1. **Paired repeated-run trial on a frozen ticket set** (evaluation). Both arms replay the same backlog snapshot k times; per-ticket paired differences, clustered by parent; run count from measured s.d. Backed by EV-01, EV-02, EV-06, EV-07. Best grade A.
2. **Harness-frozen model comparison** (evaluation). Prompts, tools, budgets, tiers pinned by a config hash; only the model varies; the reverse trial (model pinned, one role variant) uses the same design. EV-04, EV-05. B.
3. **Cost-normalised Pareto reporting** (evaluation). Every arm plotted as proxy $ per merged ticket against merge rate (and pass^k); the Pareto set, not the top score, is the result; token counts kept for re-pricing. EV-22, EV-23, EV-24, and Cost-of-Pass. B.
4. **Reliability over repetition** (evaluation). pass^k from repeated dispatch of the same tickets as the unattended-pipeline figure. EV-03. B.
5. **Live, date-stamped, audited ticket set** (evaluation). Tickets filed after model cutoffs, audited for leaked solutions and weak tests, with the exclusion list published. EV-17, EV-19, EV-25. B.
6. **Trivial-baseline arm** (evaluation and orchestration). An arm with no reviewer (merge on CI green) run beside the tiered-reviewer arm to price what review buys. EV-16. B.
7. **Escaped-defect record as the quality outcome** (evaluation). Reverts, follow-up fixes, reopened issues and static-analysis deltas tracked for months after merge, so throughput gains are checked against the quality tail. EV-11, EV-18, EV-26. A.
8. **Field-metric telemetry with stratification** (evaluation). Merge rate, time to close, review rounds and comments, PR size, rejection reason code, stratified by ticket type and review tier and by who merged. EV-12 to EV-15, EV-27, EV-30. A.
9. **Verifier-selected parallel attempts** (orchestration). Several workers on one ticket, candidates that break existing tests discarded, a scorer picks among survivors; capped at a few attempts. EV-23, EV-24. B.
10. **Ticket enrichment before dispatch** (orchestration). A pass that writes requirements and interface notes into the ticket; the one scaffold change with a measured 3x effect on resolve rate. EV-21. B.
11. **Per-role effort and model routing by measured frontier** (orchestration). Effort level and model chosen per role from the Pareto record rather than defaulting to the strongest. EV-22. B.
12. **Consumer-repo trial** (evaluation). The trial runs on a repo the plugin serves, not on the plugin's own checkout. EV-28. C.

## Open questions

- No source measures an orchestrator of parallel workers plus tiered reviewers end to end; every variance, harness and cost figure above is for a single agent on a single task. Whether between-run s.d. is larger for a multi-agent pipeline (more sampling points) is unmeasured.
- The number of repeated runs orchestrate can afford is the binding constraint: Bjarnason et al. needed 10 runs on 500 tasks to resolve 2 points; a 30-ticket backlog repeated 10 times is 300 worker runs per arm. No source gives a cheaper design for small task sets beyond paired differences and Bayesian intervals.
- Field merge rates disagree by 30 points across samples of the same agent, and the strongest predictor is who merges. No source separates "merged by a third-party maintainer after review" as a metric, which is the one orchestrate would need for a credible acceptance claim.
- Escaped defects: UTBoost and the Cursor study show the mechanism, but no source gives a rate of post-merge defects per agentic PR with a human comparison; the Faros and CodeRabbit figures are telemetry associations without controls.
- Contamination for private repos: SWE-bench Pro's public/commercial gap bounds it from above, but there is no method in these sources for estimating how much of a worker's success on a live private backlog is recall of similar public code.
- DORA 2025's throughput/instability finding could not be verified beyond the "amplifier" sentence on dora.dev; its figures are left out.
- The SWE-bench Verified annotation details (annotator count, 0-3 severity rubric, sample of 1,699) live in an OpenAI post this session could not fetch; only the 500-task fact and the 477-task OpenAI subset are confirmed from other primary pages.

## What a credible self-measurement of orchestrate would need

Stated only as far as the sources support it.

- **Unit and pairing.** The unit is a ticket outcome. Both arms run on the same frozen backlog snapshot (EV-07); outcomes are paired per ticket and standard errors clustered by parent issue (EV-06).
- **Trial count.** Repeated runs per arm, with the count set from the observed between-run s.d. on that backlog. By Bjarnason et al.'s table, 2-point resolution at s.d. 1.5 to 1.8 points needs 9 to 13 runs per arm; a 10-point effect needs one. For a backlog of tens of tickets the Bowyer et al. warning applies and intervals should be Beta/Wilson, declared wide (EV-02, EV-08). `MIN_MERGED = 20` is not a power threshold.
- **Frozen harness.** One plugin version and config hash across arms; a model trial changes only the model, a prompt trial only the prompt (EV-04, EV-05).
- **Metrics.** Primary: merge rate and pass^k over repeats (EV-03, EV-12); proxy $ per merged ticket and tokens per role, reported as a Pareto point (EV-22). Secondary: review rounds and comments per PR, PR size, time to close, rejection reason code (EV-13, EV-26, EV-27). Quality tail: escaped defects (reverts, follow-up fixes, reopened issues) and static-analysis/complexity deltas tracked for months (EV-11, EV-18).
- **Controls.** A trivial arm (CI-green merge, no reviewer) prices the reviewer tiers (EV-16). Ticket mix stratified by type and S/M/L tier, with `compare` declaring inconclusive on a mix shift (EV-15, EV-20). Tickets audited for leaked solutions and weak tests; exclusions published (EV-17, EV-25). Who merged recorded; operator self-merges counted separately (EV-14). Trial run on a consumer repo, not on claude-orchestrate itself (EV-28). No reliance on operator impression (EV-09).
- **Claim.** With that design, orchestrate can report a direction with an interval and a Pareto position on its own backlog. The sources do not support generalising from one repo, importing a literature effect size (EV-29), or comparing to public leaderboard scores (EV-19).
